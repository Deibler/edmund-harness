---
name: diagnose-from-photo
description: Use when someone sends a photo of a bug, rash, plant, broken part, or unknown object and asks what it is or what's wrong with it — give a specific best-guess identification plus the actionable next step, not a hedge-everything survey of possibilities.
---

This applies whenever a message pairs one or more photos with a bare identification question — "what is this," "what kind of bug/spider is this," "why does this look bad," "what's wrong with it" — about a physical thing (creature, plant, skin mark, damaged part, unfamiliar object).

1. Treat all photos sent together as one case, not separate questions. Multiple angles of the same subject (e.g. three plant photos) are evidence to cross-reference, not three independent asks.

2. Lead with ONE specific, named best guess — species name, part name, condition name — not a bulleted list of five possibilities framed as equally likely. Someone asking "what is this" wants an answer, not a taxonomy lecture. A confident wrong-but-correctable guess is more useful than a well-hedged non-answer; you can revise if corrected.

3. If real ambiguity remains after looking closely, still give the best guess, then name the ONE discriminating detail that would confirm or rule it out (e.g. "check if the underside has a web pattern," "get a close-up of the leaf's underside") and ask for exactly that follow-up. Don't just say "could be several things, hard to tell."

4. Immediately follow the ID with the actual reason they sent the photo — that's almost always unstated but implied:
   - Bug/spider/animal: is it dangerous (venomous, bite/sting risk), should it be removed or left alone
   - Plant: what's actually wrong (underwatering, too much/little sun, pest, root issue) and the specific fix
   - Mark or condition on a person's body: give the best guess, but flag plainly if it's something that warrants an in-person medical look — don't diagnose with false confidence, and don't bury a real caution under reassurance
   - Broken or damaged part: what the part is called, whether it's a standard/replaceable component, and how to source or repair it

5. Keep the reply short: identification, one line of reasoning (what feature led to that call), then the next step. Skip padding and disclaimers beyond what's needed.

Gotcha: a prior case (chat49, spider ID) required two follow-up complaints — "I feel like it still hasn't said what spider it is" — because the reply talked around the identification instead of naming it. Naming the thing is the deliverable; everything else is secondary.
